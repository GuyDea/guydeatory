Imagine a drop of water. Split it in half, then in half again, and again. For a long time, each
tiny drop is still water. But in the end you reach the smallest bit of water there is: one water
**molecule**.

Split that molecule, and it stops being water. You get two hydrogen [[atom|atoms]] and one oxygen
atom.

## What is a molecule?

A molecule is a group of atoms that hold on to each other. The hold between two atoms is called a
[[chemical-bond|chemical bond]]. Water, the air, sugar and the plastic in your toys are all made of
molecules. So is much of your own body.

<Analogy title="Letters and words">
Think of atoms as the letters of the alphabet, and molecules as words. With only 26 letters, you
can write every word in the dictionary. In the same way, fewer than a hundred kinds of atoms join
up into millions of different molecules.
</Analogy>

## How do you read a chemical formula?

Chemists write molecules as short codes called **chemical formulas**. Water is H₂O, and you say it
“H-two-O”. Here is how to read it:

- Each symbol stands for one kind of atom, called a [[chemical-element|chemical element]]. A symbol
  is a capital letter, sometimes with a small letter after it: H is hydrogen, O is oxygen and Cl is
  chlorine.
- A small number after a symbol tells how many of those atoms there are. H₂ means two hydrogen
  atoms.
- No number means one. So the O in H₂O is one oxygen atom.

Some symbols come from Latin names. Gold is Au, from *aurum*, and sodium is Na, from *natrium*.

## Can a molecule be made of one kind of atom?

Yes! The oxygen you breathe is O₂: two oxygen atoms joined together. Nitrogen, the main gas in the
air, is N₂.

Most molecules mix different kinds of atoms, like water (H₂O) or carbon dioxide (CO₂). A substance
made of two or more kinds of atoms joined together is called a **compound**.

The same atoms can make very different molecules. Two oxygen atoms make the oxygen we need to live.
Three oxygen atoms make ozone, O₃, a sharp-smelling gas that is harmful to breathe. High up in the
sky, though, a layer of ozone shields us from the Sun’s harmful ultraviolet light.

## How big is a molecule?

Molecules are tiny. More than three million water molecules, lined up side by side, would fit
across one millimetre.

<FunFact>
There are more water molecules in one glass of water than there are glasses of water in all the
oceans on Earth.
</FunFact>

Some molecules are much bigger than others:

| Molecule | How many atoms |
|---|---|
| water, H₂O | 3 |
| table sugar, C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ | 45 |
| haemoglobin, which carries oxygen in your blood | about 9,000 |
| DNA, which carries the instructions for your body | billions |

DNA molecules are the giants of your body. The longest one in most of your cells would be about as
long as a grown-up’s finger if you stretched it out. Yet it is only about 2 millionths of a
millimetre thick.

## Do molecules keep still?

Never! In a gas, like steam, molecules zoom around and bump into each other. In a liquid, like
water, they slide past each other. Even in solid ice, they jiggle in place.

The warmer something is, the faster its molecules move. That is what [[temperature]] measures.

When ice melts or water boils, the molecules only change how they move and how close they are.
Each one stays whole: it is still H₂O. To make new molecules, you need a
[[chemical-reaction|chemical reaction]].

## Is everything made of molecules?

No. Some things are built in other ways:

- **Salt** is a crystal of [[ion|ions]]: plus sodium ions and minus chloride ions, packed in a huge
  grid. Its formula, NaCl, only tells you that there is one sodium for every chlorine.
- **Metals**, like iron or copper, are rows of atoms held together by a shared sea of electrons.
- **Noble gases**, like the helium in party balloons, are single atoms that almost never join with
  anything.

<GoDeeper title="The exact definition">
IUPAC, the international body that sets the rules for chemistry, defines a molecule as an
electrically neutral particle made of more than one atom. A group of atoms that carries a charge,
like the hydroxide ion OH⁻, is called a polyatomic ion instead.

In the theory of gases, single atoms of noble gases are sometimes called “monatomic molecules”,
because they behave like the particles of any other gas. For salts, chemists count **formula
units** instead of molecules: one formula unit of sodium chloride is one Na⁺ and one Cl⁻. For
metals, they count atoms.
</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Counting molecules: the mole">
Molecules are so small that chemists count them in huge batches. One **mole** is exactly
6.022 140 76 × 10²³ particles, a number called the Avogadro constant. A mole of water weighs about
18 grams, a little more than a tablespoonful.

A glass of 250 millilitres holds about 14 moles of water: about 8 × 10²⁴ molecules. All the oceans
together hold about 1.3 billion cubic kilometres of water, or about 5 × 10²¹ such glasses. So the
glass has more than a thousand times as many molecules as the oceans have glasses.

Human chromosome 1, the longest DNA molecule in our cells, has about 249 million pairs of building
blocks. Stretched out, it would be about 8.5 centimetres long and 2 nanometres wide. All the DNA in
one cell together would stretch about 2 metres.
</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Where the letters-and-words picture breaks">
- Words are flat rows of letters, but molecules are 3D shapes, and the shape matters as much as the
  atoms. Read how shapes form in [[chemical-bond|how atoms join into molecules]].
- The same atoms can be joined in a different order, a bit like anagrams. Ethanol (the alcohol in
  drinks) and dimethyl ether (a gas used in some spray cans) are both C₂H₆O. Molecules like these
  are called **isomers**. A **structural formula**, such as H–O–H for water, shows which atom is
  joined to which.
- One molecule on its own is not wet, and it cannot boil or freeze. Those are things that huge
  crowds of molecules do together.
</GoDeeper>

<Remember>
- A molecule is a group of atoms held together by chemical bonds.
- A formula like H₂O tells which atoms are in a molecule, and how many of each.
- Some molecules have only one kind of atom (O₂). Compounds have two or more kinds (H₂O, CO₂).
- Molecules are tiny: more than three million water molecules fit across one millimetre.
- Salt, metals and noble gases are not made of molecules.
</Remember>
