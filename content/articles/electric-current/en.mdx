Flip a switch, and a lamp lights up at once. Something rushes through the wire — but you can’t see
it, hear it or smell it. What is it? Let’s find out, starting from the very beginning.

## What is moving?

Everything around you is built from tiny pieces called [[atom|atoms]]. Around the middle of every
atom move even tinier particles: [[electron|electrons]].

In metals, like the copper inside a cable, some electrons are not tied to one atom. They wander
freely between the atoms, like a crowd of people in a big hall.

Now give them all a push in the same direction. The whole crowd starts to drift along the wire,
together. **That drift is electric current**: electrons flowing through a wire.

## What pushes them?

A battery or a socket pushes the electrons. We call that push [[voltage]].

Electrons carry a negative [[electric-charge|charge]]. A battery crowds electrons at its minus end
and leaves too few at its plus end. The crowded electrons are pushed away from the minus end,
through the wire, towards the plus end.

## It only flows in a loop

Electrons can only flow if they have a complete path to travel around and back to the battery.
This loop is called an [[electric-circuit|electric circuit]].

A switch is a small bridge in the loop. Close it, and the loop is complete, so current flows. Open
it, and there is a gap. The electrons stop, and the lamp goes dark.

## Why does the lamp light up at once?

Here is a surprise: each electron moves very slowly — often more slowly than a snail! So how can a
lamp light up the moment you flip the switch?

<Analogy title="A tube full of marbles">
Imagine a long tube packed full of marbles. Push one marble in at one end, and at that very moment
another marble pops out at the other end. The first marble didn’t travel all the way; the push did.
</Analogy>

<Figure caption="The wire is already full of electrons. The push travels through them almost instantly." wide>
  <MarbleTube />
</Figure>

A wire is like that tube: it is already full of electrons. When the push starts, electrons all along
the loop start moving at almost the same moment. So the lamp lights up at once.

## What can current do?

When current flows, it carries [[energy]] from the battery or the power station to whatever is in
the loop. That energy can be turned into:

- **light** — in lamps and screens,
- **heat** — in kettles, toasters and irons,
- **movement** — in fans, drills and electric cars,
- **sound** — in speakers and headphones.

How fast a device uses that energy is its [[electric-power|power]]. Some materials make it harder
for current to flow; that is called [[electrical-resistance|resistance]], and it turns some of the
energy into heat.

## How strong is a current?

We measure current in **amperes**, or **amps** for short, written **A**. The name comes from the
French scientist André-Marie Ampère.

| What | About how much current |
|---|---|
| An LED lamp | 0.04 A |
| A phone charging | 1–2 A |
| A kettle | 9 A |
| A bolt of lightning | 30,000 A |

Want to know how these numbers are worked out? Read [[calculating-electric-current|how we calculate
electric current]]. And to see what current does in bigger circuits, read
[[how-electric-current-behaves|how electric current behaves]].

<Safety>
Your body can carry current too — and that is dangerous. A current of just 0.03 amps flowing
through your chest can stop your heart from beating properly. Never touch sockets, open wires or
power lines, and never use electrical things with wet hands. Batteries of 1.5 or 9 volts are
safe for experiments — with two rules. Never join a battery’s two ends with just a wire and nothing
else in the loop: the wire and the battery quickly get hot. And never put a battery in your mouth:
small round button batteries are very dangerous if swallowed.
</Safety>

<GoDeeper title="Which way does current flow?">
Electrons flow from the minus end of a battery to the plus end. But on circuit diagrams, current is
drawn flowing the other way, from plus to minus. This “conventional current” was chosen in the
1700s, before anyone knew about electrons. Scientists guessed that something positive was moving.
Both descriptions give the same answers in calculations, so the old habit stayed. Read more in the
[[history-of-electricity|history of electricity]].
</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="How slow are the electrons, really?">
In a household cable carrying a few amps, electrons drift forward at only about a tenth of a
millimetre per second. They also jiggle randomly at enormous speeds, but those random movements
cancel out. What travels fast is the push: an electric field that spreads along the wire at a large
fraction of the speed of light. In a wall socket, the current also swings back and forth 50 times every
second — that is [[alternating-current|alternating current]] — so the electrons mostly shuffle back
and forth on the spot.
</GoDeeper>

<Remember>
- Electric current is a flow of electrons through a wire.
- A battery or a socket gives the push, called voltage.
- Current only flows around a closed loop: a circuit.
- Current carries energy that becomes light, heat, movement or sound.
- Current is measured in amperes (A). Mains current can kill.
</Remember>
