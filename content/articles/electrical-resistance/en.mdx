Try drinking a thick milkshake through a thin straw. You suck as hard as you can, but only a little
comes up. The straw holds the milkshake back.

Electricity gets held back too, as it flows through wires, lamps and toasters. This holding back is
called **resistance**. Let’s find out where it comes from, and why it makes things hot.

## What holds electricity back?

Electricity in a wire is a flow of tiny particles called [[electron|electrons]]. A push called
[[voltage]] makes them drift along the wire, all together. This flow is the
[[electric-current|electric current]].

But a wire is not an empty tube. It is packed with metal [[atom|atoms]], sitting in neat rows. The
atoms are never still: they jiggle all the time, even in a cold wire.

As the electrons drift along, they keep bumping into the jiggling atoms. Every bump slows them down.
How strongly something holds back the flow is called its **resistance**.

## Like a narrow pipe

<Analogy>
Think of electricity as water flowing through pipes. The same pump pushes water into a wide pipe and
into a narrow one. Lots of water gushes through the wide pipe, but only a trickle squeezes through
the narrow one. A pipe clogged with sand holds the water back too.
</Analogy>

<Figure caption="The same push sends lots of water through a wide pipe, but only a trickle through a narrow one. A narrow pipe is like a path with a high resistance." wide>
  <NarrowPipe />
</Figure>

Electricity works the same way. With the same push, a lot of current flows through something with a
low resistance. Only a little flows through something with a high resistance.

## What makes resistance bigger or smaller?

Four things decide how much a wire holds electricity back:

- **The material.** Copper and silver let electrons through very easily, so electric cables are made
  of copper. Other metals, such as iron, hold electrons back more. Plastic and rubber hold them back
  so much that almost nothing flows: they are [[conductors-and-insulators|insulators]].
- **The length.** In a long wire, the electrons have more atoms to get past. A wire twice as long
  has twice the resistance.
- **The thickness.** A thick wire has more room, like a wide corridor where many people can walk
  side by side. The thicker the wire, the lower its resistance.
- **The [[temperature]].** When metal gets hotter, its atoms jiggle harder. The electrons bump into
  them more often, so the resistance goes up.

<FunFact>
Some materials lose their resistance completely when they get cold enough. In 1911, a scientist
cooled the metal mercury to about −269 °C, and its resistance suddenly vanished. Such materials are
called superconductors. Hospitals use them in MRI scanners, the big machines that take pictures of
the inside of the body.
</FunFact>

## How do we measure resistance?

Resistance is measured in **ohms**. The short sign for an ohm is **Ω**, the Greek letter omega. The
unit is named after Georg Ohm, a German scientist. He found the rule that connects voltage, current
and resistance: [[ohms-law|Ohm’s law]].

| What | Resistance |
|---|---|
| 1 metre of copper wire in a house cable | about 0.01 Ω |
| The heating part of a 2,000-watt kettle | about 26 Ω |
| An old-style 60-watt light bulb, while it glows | about 900 Ω |
| A piece of plastic the size of a sugar cube | more than a million million Ω |

A small part whose only job is to have a certain resistance is called a **resistor**. Almost every
electronic gadget has resistors inside.

## Resistance makes heat

Every time an electron bumps into an atom, the atom jiggles a little harder. And faster jiggling
atoms mean a hotter material. So resistance turns electrical [[energy]] into [[heat]].

Sometimes that heat is exactly what we want. A toaster, a kettle and a hair dryer all contain wires
with a high resistance. When current flows through them, they get hot — hot enough to toast bread or
boil water.

An old-style light bulb works the same way. Its very thin wire, called a filament, gets hotter than
2,000 °C, so hot that it glows white. But only a tiny part of the energy becomes light. Almost all
of it becomes heat, which is why these bulbs waste so much electricity.

At other times, the heat is a waste. Cables, chargers and laptops get warm while they work. To keep
this waste small, cables are made of copper. Cables for things that use lots of electricity, such as
an electric cooker, are extra thick.

How fast something uses up electrical energy is called its [[electric-power|power]].

<Safety>
Toasters, kettles, irons and heaters get very hot. Never touch their hot parts.

Never poke anything into a toaster, not even a fork to free stuck bread. Ask a grown-up for help
instead. Don’t plug lots of powerful devices into one extension lead, because its cable can overheat
and start a fire.
</Safety>

<GoDeeper title="The formula for a wire’s resistance">
The resistance of a wire depends on its material, its length and its thickness:

$$
R = \rho \cdot \frac{L}{A}
$$

- $R$ is the resistance, in ohms (Ω).
- $\rho$ (the Greek letter rho) is the **resistivity** of the material: how strongly the material
  itself holds electrons back. Copper has about $1.7 \times 10^{-8}\ \Omega \cdot \text{m}$
  (ohm-metres). Nichrome, a mix of nickel and chromium used in heating elements, has about 65 times
  more.
- $L$ is the length of the wire, in metres (m).
- $A$ is the area of the wire’s cross-section (the size of its cut end), in square metres (m²).

For example, 1 metre of copper wire with a cross-section of 1.5 mm²
($1.5 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2$), a common size in house cables, has a resistance of:

$$
R = 1.7 \times 10^{-8}\ \Omega \cdot \text{m} \cdot \frac{1\ \text{m}}{1.5 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2} \approx 0.011\ \Omega
$$

A wire twice as long has twice the resistance. A wire twice as wide has a cut end four times as big,
so its resistance is only a quarter.
</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="What really slows the electrons down">
- In a metal, every atom lets go of one or two of its outer electrons. These free electrons wander
  through the whole metal. The atoms, now called ions, stay in a regular pattern called a crystal.
- Electrons bouncing off atoms like balls is a simplified picture. Quantum physics shows that a
  perfectly regular crystal whose atoms stood perfectly still would hardly hold electrons back at
  all. What scatters them are the vibrations of the atoms, which grow with temperature, and flaws in
  the crystal: missing atoms, atoms of other elements and the borders between tiny crystals.
- That is why the resistance of copper rises by about 0.4 % for every degree Celsius, and why mixes
  of metals, such as nichrome, resist far more than pure metals.
- Not every material behaves like a metal. In many thermistors (small parts used in electronic
  thermometers), the resistance goes *down* as they warm up.
</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Where the pipe picture goes wrong">
- Water rubs against the walls of a pipe, so it is held back mostly near the walls. In a wire, the
  whole metal holds electrons back, all the way across. The pipe picture gets the result right —
  longer and thinner means more resistance — but not the reason.
- Resistance does not use up electrons. Every second, as many electrons flow out of a toaster’s
  heating wire as flow into it. What the electrons lose on the way is energy, and it becomes heat.
</GoDeeper>

<Remember>
- Resistance is how much something holds back the flow of electricity.
- It is like a narrow or clogged pipe for water.
- Long, thin wires resist more than short, thick ones. Copper resists very little.
- Resistance is measured in ohms (Ω).
- Resistance turns electrical energy into heat: useful in a toaster, wasted in a cable.
</Remember>
