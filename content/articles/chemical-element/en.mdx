A piece of pure gold, the helium in a party balloon, a sparkling diamond: all of them are made of
[[atom|atoms]]. But gold atoms are different from helium atoms, and helium atoms are different
from the atoms in a diamond. Atoms come in different kinds, and each kind is a **chemical
element**. Let’s find out what makes each kind special.

## What is an element?

Some substances are made of only one kind of atom. Pure gold contains only gold atoms, and the
helium that makes balloons float contains only helium atoms. A diamond is made of only carbon atoms.
A substance like this is called a **chemical element**, or an element for short.

Most things around you are not elements. They are made of two or more kinds of atoms, joined
together. Water, for example, is made of hydrogen atoms and oxygen atoms, joined into tiny groups
called [[molecule|molecules]]. A substance made of several elements joined like this is called a
**compound**.

You can break water apart into hydrogen and oxygen, for example with electricity. But no chemical
trick can break hydrogen or oxygen into anything simpler. That is what makes them elements: they
are the simplest substances of all.

## What makes each element different?

Every atom has a tiny nucleus in its middle, and the nucleus holds protons. The number of protons
decides which element the atom belongs to:

- Every atom with 1 proton is hydrogen.
- Every atom with 2 protons is helium.
- Every atom with 6 protons is carbon.
- Every atom with 8 protons is oxygen.

This number is called the **atomic number**. Every element has its own, from 1 for hydrogen all the
way up to 118. No two elements share a number, and no number is missing.

<Analogy title="Counting wheels">
Think of bikes. One wheel makes a unicycle, two wheels make a bicycle, and three wheels make a
tricycle. The wheels are all alike: only their number decides what the bike is called.

Protons work the same way. All protons are alike, and their number decides the element. Give a
carbon atom one more proton, and it turns into nitrogen!
</Analogy>

An atom can gain or lose a few [[electron|electrons]]. It can also have a few more or fewer
neutrons. It still stays the same element, just as a bicycle with a basket or a bell is still a
bicycle.

Atoms of one element with different numbers of neutrons are called [[isotope|isotopes]]. Atoms with
too many or too few electrons are called [[ion|ions]].

<GoDeeper title="Atomic number and mass number">
- The **atomic number** $Z$ is the number of protons in the nucleus. It alone defines the element.
- The **mass number** $A$ is the number of protons and neutrons together. Carbon-12 has $A = 12$
  (6 protons and 6 neutrons), and carbon-14 has $A = 14$ (6 protons and 8 neutrons).
- The number of electrons can change without changing the element. An atom that loses or gains
  electrons becomes an ion, but it keeps its atomic number.
- Only nuclear processes change $Z$: [[radioactivity|radioactive]] decay, nuclear reactions inside
  stars, or collisions in particle accelerators. Chemistry cannot turn lead into gold, as the
  alchemists once hoped. Nuclear physics can: in 2025, scientists at CERN reported that their
  particle collider turns some lead nuclei into gold nuclei. From 2015 to 2018 it made about
  86 billion of them: in all, only about 29 trillionths of a gram. Each one hit the collider’s walls
  and broke apart within a split second.
</GoDeeper>

## A short name for every element

Writing long names again and again would take ages. So every element has a short **symbol** of one
or two letters. The first letter is always a capital, and the second is always small.

| Element | Symbol |
|---|---|
| hydrogen | H |
| helium | He |
| carbon | C |
| oxygen | O |
| iron | Fe |
| gold | Au |
| sodium | Na |

Some symbols look strange. Why is iron Fe, and why is gold Au? These symbols come from the
elements’ Latin names: *ferrum* for iron, *aurum* for gold and *natrium* for sodium.

Scientists all over the world use the same symbols, whatever language they speak. Chemists in
Slovakia and chemists in Japan both write Au for gold.

All the elements are arranged in a famous chart, the [[periodic-table|periodic table]]. It puts
elements that behave alike next to each other.

## How many elements are there?

Scientists know 118 elements. Of these, 94 are found in nature on Earth, though a few of them only
in tiny traces. Scientists have made the rest, often only a handful of atoms at a time. The heaviest
of them fall apart in less than a second, because they are [[radioactivity|radioactive]].

The newest four got their names in 2016: nihonium, moscovium, tennessine and oganesson. Element 118,
oganesson, is the heaviest element known so far.

<FunFact>
Some elements are named after places, like polonium (after Poland) and americium (after America).
Others honour scientists: curium is named after Marie and Pierre Curie, and einsteinium after
Albert Einstein.
</FunFact>

<GoDeeper title="How many are natural, exactly?">
- Elements 1 to 94 have all been found in nature. Six of them occur only in extremely tiny traces:
  technetium (43), promethium (61), astatine (85), francium (87), neptunium (93) and plutonium
  (94). Natural radioactive decay and rare natural nuclear reactions keep making small amounts.
- Only 80 elements have at least one stable isotope, one that never decays. Every element heavier
  than lead (82) is radioactive, although some decay extremely slowly. Bismuth-209 has a half-life
  of about $2 \times 10^{19}$ years, more than a billion times the age of the universe.
- IUPAC, the world body that looks after chemical names, announced on 30 December 2015 that the
  discoveries of elements 113, 115, 117 and 118 had been verified. That completed the seventh row
  of the periodic table. IUPAC approved their names in November 2016.
</GoDeeper>

## Which elements are the most common?

Some elements are everywhere, and others are very rare.

| Where | The most common elements |
|---|---|
| the whole universe | hydrogen and helium |
| the Earth’s crust | oxygen and silicon |
| your body | oxygen, carbon and hydrogen |

Hydrogen is by far the most common element in the universe: more than 9 of every 10 atoms are
hydrogen atoms. Stars like our Sun are made mostly of hydrogen and helium.

In the rocks and sand under your feet, almost half of the weight is oxygen. You can’t see it,
because it is locked up together with other elements, such as silicon.

About two-thirds of your body’s weight is oxygen, mostly in the water inside you. A child who
weighs 30&nbsp;kilograms carries almost 20&nbsp;kilograms of oxygen!

<GoDeeper title="The numbers">
- **The universe** (ordinary matter), by mass: hydrogen about 74%, helium about 24%, and all the
  other elements together only about 2%. Counted by atoms, it is about 92% hydrogen and 8% helium.
  These two were made in the first minutes after the Big Bang. Almost all heavier elements were
  made later, inside stars.
- **The Earth’s crust**, by mass: oxygen 46%, silicon 28%, aluminium 8% and iron 6%, followed by
  calcium, sodium, magnesium and potassium.
- **The human body**, by mass: oxygen 65%, carbon 18%, hydrogen 10%, nitrogen 3%, calcium 1.5% and
  phosphorus 1%. These six elements make up about 99% of your mass. Counted by atoms, hydrogen
  wins: about 62% of the atoms in your body are hydrogen atoms, because they are so light.
</GoDeeper>

## Elements join up

With only 118 elements, how can there be millions of different substances? Because elements
combine. Their atoms hold on to each other with [[chemical-bond|chemical bonds]], and each new
combination makes something new.

The new substance can be completely different from its elements! Sodium is a soft, shiny metal that
fizzes wildly in water. Chlorine is a poisonous, yellowish-green gas. Joined together, they make
table salt: the white crystals you sprinkle on your food.

When atoms let go of old partners and join new ones, we call it a
[[chemical-reaction|chemical reaction]].

<Remember>
- An element is a substance made of only one kind of atom.
- The number of protons in an atom decides which element it is. This is its atomic number.
- Every element has a symbol of one or two letters, like H, O, Fe or Au.
- Scientists know 118 elements, and 94 of them are found in nature.
- Elements join together into countless compounds, like water and salt.
</Remember>
