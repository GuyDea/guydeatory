Is today a jacket day or a T-shirt day? Is the bath too hot? Do you have a fever? To answer questions
like these, we need to know the **temperature**: how hot or cold something is.

## What makes something hot or cold?

Everything is made of tiny particles: [[atom|atoms]], and little groups of atoms stuck together.
They are far too small to see, even with an ordinary microscope.

These particles never stop moving. In a solid, like an ice cube or a spoon, they jiggle in place. In
a liquid, like water, they jiggle and slide past each other. In a gas, like air, they zoom around
and bump into each other.

When something gets warmer, its particles jiggle faster. When it cools down, they slow down. So
temperature tells how fast the particles are jiggling: the faster they jiggle, the higher the
temperature.

<FunFact>
The particles of air in your room zoom around at about 500 metres every second. That is faster than
a jet plane! But they crash into each other billions of times a second, so they never get far.
</FunFact>

## How do we measure temperature?

We measure temperature with a **thermometer**. Many thermometers are thin glass tubes with a
coloured liquid inside.

When the liquid warms up, it takes up a little more space, so it climbs up the tube. When it cools,
it shrinks back down. The numbers on the side show the temperature.

Many digital thermometers have a tiny electronic part at the tip instead. Electricity flows through it
more easily or less easily, depending on how warm it is. A small chip turns this into a number on
the screen.

<Safety>
Very old glass thermometers may have a silvery liquid inside. It is mercury, which is poisonous. If
one breaks, do not touch it, and call a grown-up at once.
</Safety>

## Degrees Celsius

In most countries, we measure temperature in **degrees Celsius**, written **°C**. The scale uses
water as its guide:

- At **0 °C**, water freezes into ice, and ice melts.
- At **100 °C**, water boils, at least at sea level.

On a high mountain, water boils at a lower temperature. Find out why in
[[boiling-point|why water boils at different temperatures]].

Temperatures colder than freezing get a minus sign. So −18 °C means 18 degrees below freezing.

The scale is named after Anders Celsius, a Swedish astronomer. He described it in 1742.

<FunFact>
Celsius first made his scale upside down! In his version, water boiled at 0 degrees and froze at
100. Soon after, other scientists turned it the right way round.
</FunFact>

## From the freezer to the Sun

Here are some temperatures to compare:

| What | Temperature |
|---|---|
| The coldest air ever measured on Earth, in Antarctica | about −89 °C |
| Inside a freezer | −18 °C |
| Water freezes, ice melts | 0 °C |
| Inside a fridge | about 4 °C |
| A comfortable room | about 20 °C |
| Your body | about 37 °C |
| Water boils (at sea level) | 100 °C |
| A hot oven | about 200 °C |
| The surface of the Sun | about 5,500 °C |

Your body keeps itself at about 37 °C, in summer and in winter. When you are ill, it can climb a
little higher. That is a fever.

## An experiment: metal and wood

Here is a safe experiment with a surprise. Find a metal spoon and a wooden spoon that have been
lying in the same room for at least an hour. Now they both have the same temperature as the room.

Touch them, one after the other. The metal spoon feels much colder! But a thermometer would show
the same temperature for both. How can that be?

Your hand is warmer than the room, so [[heat]] flows out of your hand into anything you touch.
Metal passes heat along very quickly. It pulls heat out of your skin fast, and your skin cools down.
Wood passes heat along slowly, so your skin stays warm.

Your skin does not feel the temperature of the thing you touch. It feels how fast it is losing heat.
Fast means "cold!", and slow means "not so cold".

<Safety>
Only do this experiment with things at room temperature, never with hot pans. In freezing weather,
never touch frozen metal with your tongue or with wet skin. It can freeze stuck to it.
</Safety>

<GoDeeper title="Degrees Fahrenheit">
In the United States, people measure temperature in degrees **Fahrenheit** (°F). On this scale,
water freezes at 32 °F and boils at 212 °F, and a healthy body is about 98.6 °F. To change degrees
Celsius into degrees Fahrenheit:

$$
F = C \cdot \frac{9}{5} + 32
$$

Here $C$ is the temperature in degrees Celsius and $F$ is the same temperature in degrees
Fahrenheit. A room at 20 °C is $20 \cdot \frac{9}{5} + 32 = 68$ °F.
</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="Kelvin and absolute zero">
Can things get colder and colder forever? No. The colder something gets, the slower its particles
jiggle, and there is a limit: **absolute zero**, at −273.15 °C. There, the particles have the
smallest jiggle possible. They cannot give away any more energy, so nothing can ever be colder.
(Quantum physics says that a tiny jiggle remains, even at absolute zero.)

Scientists often use the **kelvin** scale (K), which starts at absolute zero. One kelvin is the same
step as one degree Celsius:

$$
T_{\text{K}} = T_{\text{C}} + 273.15
$$

Here $T_{\text{C}}$ is the temperature in degrees Celsius and $T_{\text{K}}$ is the same temperature
in kelvins. Water freezes at 273.15 K, a room is about 293 K, and the surface of the Sun is about
5,800 K. Kelvins are written without the degree sign.

Nobody has ever reached absolute zero. But scientists have cooled atoms to less than a billionth of
a degree above it.
</GoDeeper>

<GoDeeper title="What temperature measures, precisely">
Temperature is linked to the *average* movement [[energy]] of the particles, not to their total
energy. That is why a bathtub of warm water holds far more heat energy than a cup of hot tea: the
tea's particles move faster, but the bath has many more of them.

Our skin is a poor thermometer. It senses its own temperature, which drops quickly when a material
draws heat away fast. Metals and stone do this strongly; wood, cork and wool hardly at all.
Engineers call this property **thermal effusivity**.
</GoDeeper>

<Remember>
- Everything is made of tiny particles that are always jiggling.
- Temperature tells how fast they jiggle: faster means hotter.
- We measure temperature with a thermometer, usually in degrees Celsius (°C).
- Water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C at sea level. Your body is about 37 °C.
- Metal feels colder than wood at the same temperature, because it pulls heat out of your skin
  faster.
</Remember>
